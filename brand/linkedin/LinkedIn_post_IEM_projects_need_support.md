In 2025, companies reported ₹5.8 lakh crore of investment at the point their plants started production. That is more than the ₹5.3 lakh crore of new plans they filed the same year.

The numbers, from DPIIT's Annual Report 2025-26 (the Industrial Entrepreneur Memorandum, IEM):

- Part A, the intention to build: 702 filings in 2025, ₹5.3 lakh crore proposed. In 2024 it was 986 filings and ₹6.0 lakh crore.
- Part B, production started: 998 filings in 2025, ₹5.8 lakh crore invested. In 2024 it was 1,165 filings and ₹4.9 lakh crore.
- Over 2021 to 2025: 5,629 Part A filings (₹28.4 lakh crore proposed) and 4,859 Part B filings (₹19.5 lakh crore invested).
- Since August 1991: 1,12,843 IEMs, with ₹160 lakh crore of proposed investment.

Part A and Part B filings are different projects filed in different years, so the two totals cannot be subtracted to give a loss rate.

Two changes make this harder to see. Since April 2025 an IEM is only for units above ₹125 crore of plant and machinery or ₹500 crore of turnover. Since October 2025, companies generate the acknowledgement themselves on the National Single Window System, with no DPIIT approval, and DPIIT is consulting on whether to eliminate IEM filings altogether.

From the company-level lists I read for 2001 to 2024, about 1 in 3 of the 2016–19 Part A filings can be traced to a Part B, against 1 in 5 in 2001–06. Where a plant is built, the median gap from filing to production is under a year. For 2021–24 filings with no Part B, most companies are still Active, so the question is where the project is stuck.

An IEM is not a licence or a clearance. After it, a project still needs land, environmental clearance where it applies, state approvals, power, water and finance. That is where a large project often needs help: a sound project report, approvals in the right order, and a lender who understands the plan.
